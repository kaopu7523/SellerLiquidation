# amazon liquidation auctions

## Amazon Bulk Liquidations Store (Beta Program) — 官方帮助页
- 链接: https://www.amazon.com/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=T0SulrJwRKDdSNHxi3
- 要点:
  - Amazon 官方目前唯一的"自营"清算购买渠道是 **Bulk Liquidations Store（Beta）**，卖的是"lot"（整批货），来源是 Amazon 自己或第三方卖家的库存，分两类：overstock（全新未拆封）或 damaged（客户退货/处理中损坏）。
  - **入驻门槛（Beta 客户要求）**：① 拥有 Amazon Business 账号；② 已加入 Amazon Tax Exemption Program (ATEP)；③ 同意 Bulk Liquidations Store 条款。目前仅限美国本土（contiguous US），未入驻的账号加不进购物车，需要先加入 waitlist。
  - 购买流程和普通 Amazon 购物一样（加购物车结账），每个 lot 详情页会写清楚数量和价格，**价格不可议价**，货品按"as is"现状销售。
  - **售后政策很硬**：不支持退货/换货/退换，只有"数量不符"或"货物损坏到无法使用"才能在收货后 7 天内联系 bulk-liquidations-beta@amazon.com 申请退款。
  - 大件订单可能整托运输（收货方需要叉车或卸货月台），Amazon 不承诺具体到货时间。

## Amazon Liquidation Auctions（B-Stock）
- 链接: https://bstock.com/auctions/amazon
- 要点:
  - B-Stock 定位为全球最大的 B2B 退货/清仓货 marketplace，连接美国前十大零售商中的 9 家（含 Amazon）与买家，Amazon Liquidation Auctions 是其中一个子板块。
  - 买家规模从"几托盘"到"整卡车"不等，覆盖几十个品类；按地区（美国各州/加拿大等）筛选具体拍卖场次。
  - 需要先注册（Register Now）才能查看/竞拍，新买家有专门的入门资源（Buyer Basics）：如何注册、竞拍新手教程、区分 Auctions / B-Stock Supply / Private Marketplaces 三种购买渠道的差异。

## YouTube 视频素材

### How to Find Profitable Target Liquidation Auctions (10 Real Deals Analyzed)
- 链接: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OHDh4N75ddg
- 发布日期: 2026-03-06
- 观看量: 9,467
- 整理内容:

**How B-Stock auctions actually work.** The creator walks through Target's live liquidation auction storefront on B-Stock (the same marketplace model Amazon's Bulk Liquidations program uses) to demonstrate a repeatable bidding process. A recurring tip: auctions that close over the weekend tend to draw fewer competing bids because "everybody else is slacking off," so weekend-closing lots can be scored cheaper than weekday ones — though the creator stresses this only matters if you're bidding on emotion rather than a pre-calculated max price.

**The listed retail/MSRP number is not trustworthy on its own.** Lot summary pages show a headline retail or MSRP value, but this figure frequently disagrees with the sum of individual item prices in the manifest — in one example a bedding lot showed a $14,261 retail price on one line and a $38,172 MSRP on another. The creator's standing rule: always download and check the manifest (the line-by-line item list) before bidding, especially for newer buyers, and treat the headline number as unreliable. A common shortcut used throughout the video is to mentally cut the listed retail value roughly in half as a more realistic ceiling for what the lot could actually resell for, then bid based on that adjusted number.

**Freight/shipping is a separate, non-negotiable cost that must be built into the bid math.** B-Stock does not set freight — the shipping carrier does — and freight can add hundreds to thousands of dollars on top of the winning bid depending on lot location and buyer location (the creator, based in California, skipped several attractive lots simply because they shipped from Maryland or Indiana and freight would have been $2,000+). The rule stated explicitly: calculate total landed cost (bid + freight) against realistic resale value before bidding; if the math doesn't work, don't bid, regardless of how cheap the win price looks.

**Worked examples with real numbers from the video:**
- 4 pallets of bedding/home goods: retail shown as $14,261 (manifest MSRP $38,172), average price at time of recording $2.64/unit, located in Ontario, CA.
- 3 pallets of backpacks/back-to-school gear, 632 units: ~$18,500 listed retail (~$29.30/unit average), landed cost after freight came out to about $2.54/unit.
- 5 pallets of footwear, 947 pairs: $40,000 retail (creator halves this to ~$20,000 as a working estimate), freight to California would have run ~$2,500, so the creator passed despite liking the deal.
- Toys/games lot, 2,217 units: $46,000 retail (halved to ~$23,000), current bid ~$1.36/unit; manifest carried a disclaimer that "sets may be missing pieces or otherwise incomplete" — a recurring risk with toy/game liquidation lots.
- Consumer electronics lot, 1,211 units: $62,661 target retail price, average bid price at the time was $4.77/unit.
- Home goods lot, 504 units: $16,698 retail, only one bid at $650 — roughly 8% of stated retail, which the creator flags as a comparatively safe/underpriced bid.
- A full truckload of furniture: $76,967 MSRP. Truckload lots cannot be delivered to a residential address and require either a loading dock or a forklift to unload, since standard tractor-trailers have no liftgate — buyers need a self-storage facility or warehouse with unloading capability.

**Practical takeaway for a seller-facing article:** this video is useful less for exact resale numbers (which are buyer-side and time-sensitive) and more for explaining, with concrete evidence, why liquidated inventory is priced the way it is on the auction side — the "cut retail in half, then subtract freight" heuristic mirrors why sellers only recover a small fraction of original value once their FBA inventory enters the liquidation channel.

### I Bought A $14,925 Amazon Return Pallet...
- 链接: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kbxf5B_67JM
- 发布日期: 2025-07-30
- 观看量: 1,536,461
- 整理内容:

**Deal structure.** The creator purchased two pallets of Amazon customer returns with a combined estimated retail value of roughly $14,925, sourced from bulk.com — notable because bulk.com was liquidating its own entire operation and closing permanently that same weekend, and this lot was discounted about 15% off its normal listing as part of that wind-down. Total cost after all fees and $300 shipping: **$3,440**. The video frames this as a real-money test of whether return pallets can turn a profit once every item is individually valued against actual recent eBay sold listings (not just an assumed markup).

**Core risk stated up front:** items in a customer-return pallet are untested. Amazon (or the retailer) has not verified that returned electronics still power on or that multi-part items are complete — a product could be a simple change-of-mind return in perfect condition, or it could be broken and returned specifically because it stopped working, with no way to tell which until the box is opened.

**Item-by-item valuations (based on the creator checking actual recent eBay sold prices, not asking prices), which illustrate real secondary-market recovery rates on liquidated electronics and general merchandise:**
- Sony PlayStation DualSense controller (tested working): valued at $54, based on a recent sold listing.
- Gemini portable Bluetooth speaker (tested working, like new): valued around $70 — for comparison, brand-new units sell for $169 and pre-owned units around $140.
- Giant yard beer pong set: valued at ~$24 (brand-new retail is $60).
- A side-table base missing its matching tabletop (the other carton was never included): valued at $0 — flagged as effectively unsellable without the missing piece, a common issue with multi-carton furniture items in return pallets.
- Westinghouse 49" dual-FHD ultrawide monitor: secondary-market value around $550 — the creator notes this competes with monitors like the Samsung Odyssey that typically retail near $1,000+.
- Thrustmaster racing wheel/pedal set (PS5-compatible): retails new at $599, ~$450 on the secondary market — but the creator points out this number is misleading in isolation because the accessory requires a $500–600 PlayStation 5 to actually use, making the realistic "cost to use" closer to $1,200 all-in.
- Two Polo Ralph Lauren area rugs: combined original retail $416; realistic resale valued at ~$150 total for the pair.
- Working chainsaws (multiple units, tested by cutting through the box): kept for personal/resale use, demonstrated fully functional.
- Samsung Odyssey Ark curved gaming monitor (the single highest-value item in the entire haul): brand new retail is $2,000–$2,600; pre-owned units sell around $1,500 on eBay. Despite being a return, the unit was effectively unused (screen protector still on, full original packaging) and was conservatively valued at **$1,600**.
- Hoverboard (tested working, visibly used): valued at $30.
- Insta360 8K 360° action camera bundle: retail $521 new, ~$420 on eBay resale.
- Gaming/streamer chair (assembled and tested): retail $99, valued at ~$60 used.
- Sealed LEGO Speed Champions Ferrari F1 set: retail $25, valued at ~$15 resale (kept unopened).
- Two Elk Home lighting fixtures (sold-out model): original retail ~$350 each; realistic resale valued at $50 each ($100 total) given the lower demand for niche home-decor items.
- Sealed dual 12" subwoofer enclosure + amplifier (car audio): retails new for $400 (confirmed against the current Amazon listing price) — valued at $250 as a "steal price" to move it quickly.
- Sony A90K 48" OLED TV: retail $1,400 — arrived **physically damaged and non-functional** (would not power on, likely dropped in transit), valued at **$0**. Explicitly called out as the clearest example of return-pallet risk: a high-ticket item that is a total loss.
- Vizio 40"-class TV (1080p, not 4K): tested working, retails around $138 at Walmart, valued at $50 resale.
- Five Safavieh bar stools: $246 each at retail; valued at ~$150 each for a quick resale (about 61% of retail).
- A stackable pantry cabinet (fully assembled and inspected): retails at $197 (Home Depot/Amazon), valued at $75.
- Nintendo Switch 2 (Mario Kart World bundle) — the final and highest-anticipation item: tested fully functional, valued at $489 based on the last actual eBay sold price (close to its $499 retail), reflecting strong resale demand for still-in-demand consumer electronics even as used/returned units.

**Bottom line for a seller-facing article:** running total of realistic (not optimistic) resale value across both pallets came to **$5,172** against a **$3,440** total cost, for an estimated **$1,732 profit** — roughly a 50% return, but only after each item was individually researched, tested, and priced against real recent sold comps, with at least one high-value item (the $1,400 TV) turning into a total write-off. This is useful evidence for a seller-facing piece on liquidation economics: it shows concretely why liquidation buyers price defensively (bidding well under retail), which is the direct reason sellers only recover a fraction of original value when their own excess/returned FBA inventory flows into this same channel.
